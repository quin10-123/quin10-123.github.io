About the team

Forze Hydrogen Racing is a student team from Delft University of Technology. Every year, a new team of around 50 students develops a hydrogen-electric race car, with help from alumni, partners and sponsors. The car is powered by a fuel cell, which turns hydrogen into electricity, with water as the only emission.

Building a race car on hydrogen means solving problems that conventional race cars never face: storing hydrogen safely in a car, packaging a complete fuel cell system in a tight chassis, and combining it with a high-voltage electric drivetrain. During my time as Chief Engineer, the team developed the Forze 9.

Five departments

The team is split into five departments. As Chief Engineer, I was responsible for the four technical departments and for making sure their work came together in one car.

A startup in a student team

Working at Forze feels much like working at a startup. Every year, a new team takes over and has twelve months to develop a car. The deadlines are tight, many concepts are completely new, and there is no ready-made solution to look up: if something does not work, the team has to figure it out itself. It is hard work, often with long days, but you learn more in one year than you would in many years elsewhere.

Projects

Integrating the hydrogen system (BoP)

2023 – 2024 · Forze 9 · Vehicle integration

Getting a hydrogen system that did not even work yet into the car and running on hydrogen. Alumni expected 5 months; we did it in 6 weeks.

A fuel cell cannot run on its own. It needs a whole system around it, the Balance of Plant (BoP): Anode (hydrogen supply), Cathode (air supply), cooling and all the tubing, wiring and electronics in between. Getting this system into the car, and making it work together with everything else, was the biggest project of my year.

The starting point

We started the year with a BoP that did not work yet: the previous team had not finished building the setup. So the first step was to complete it and get it running as a stand-alone system, outside the car. Once all its functions had been tested, we moved on to small performance tests, stepping the power setpoint up and down. At the same time, we worked on the drivetrain of the car, to make sure the motors were fully functional.

The integration plan

With both the BoP and the drivetrain working, the real challenge began: integrating them into one car. The hardest part was that the car and the BoP each had their own in-house designed and built embedded system. Together they had well over 20 actuators, 70 sensors and two different finite state machines (FSMs), and all of it had to become one system.

On top of that, several things were new at the same time: our own PCBs were not yet fully stable, the wiring harness was new, and the two FSMs had to become one. If you change all of that at once and something goes wrong, you can never really tell what caused the problem. So I made an integration plan that separated these risks as much as possible, in three phases:

  1. Phase 1 · Mechanical integration

    We installed the BoP in the car, still with its own embedded system, so the car temporarily had two: one controlling the car and one controlling the BoP. The tubing had changed slightly between the testbench and the car, so we first confirmed that the BoP still worked after installation. This told us that mechanically everything was in order.

  2. Phase 2 · Embedded integration

    The hardest phase: merging the two embedded systems into one. Everything had to run from one combined FSM and one new wiring harness, on PCBs that were still not fully stable, and the whole safety net had to work properly for the combined system.

  3. Phase 3 · Powertrain integration

    Finally, we connected the high-voltage (HV) bus of the BoP to the HV bus of the car. From then on, the fuel cell supplied power to the accumulator, and from there to the motors.

The result

After phase 3, the car could spin its wheels purely on hydrogen power, without any outside help: a complete stand-alone hydrogen-electric car.

Alumni expected the integration to take 5 months. We did it in 6 weeks.

The key was preparation. The plan was thought through in great detail and had backup solutions for many of the things that could go wrong. Because we thought ahead, we spotted most problems and had a solution ready before they could cause any delay.

My part:

  • Made the integration plan for all three phases, including the backup solutions
  • Designed the architecture of the safety net
  • Designed the FSMs together with the software engineers
  • Came up with the final design of the embedded system after merging the two systems

Safety architecture

2023 – 2024 · Forze 9 · System safety

Keeping a car with high-pressure hydrogen and a high-voltage system safe for the drivers, the team and everyone around the track.

A hydrogen race car combines high-pressure hydrogen with a high-voltage electrical system. The safety architecture defines how the car detects dangerous situations, such as a hydrogen leak or an electrical fault, and how it brings itself into a safe state, so that the drivers, the team and the people around the track are protected.

My part: I was end responsible for the safety of the Forze 9. I made sure safety was designed into the car's architecture from the start, instead of added at the end, and that the car met the safety requirements needed to drive on track.

Track testing

2024 · Forze 9 · Testing

Six weeks from the first lap to race day: night tests on an airport runway, an overheating car, a broken gearbox and demo laps in Assen.

A new car only proves itself on track. Track testing is where the design, the assembly and the software come together for the first time at full speed, and where problems show up that you never see on paper.

Six weeks, and not a single lap

When the integration was finished, our first race was only six weeks away, and the car had never driven on a track. Everything we knew came from our in-house dyno. That proved that the wheels could spin at different speed and torque setpoints, and that the BoP followed along, but not much more. We had never tested the brakes, the vehicle dynamics, the aero, the cooling, vibrations, heavy loads on the drivetrain or regenerative braking. Most importantly, we had never done an endurance run, so we did not know how the BoP would cope with high power for a longer time.

All of that had to be validated in six weeks. So I set up a test campaign that built up step by step, so we could test every function of the car without risking major damage.

Test 1: electric only, at night

The first test ran without hydrogen, on the electric system only. That way we could check whether the car was structurally sound without any risk of a hydrogen leak. We put more load on the drivetrain, saw how the car handled unexpected loads, and got our first data on cornering and vehicle dynamics. We tested at Breda Airport, at night: after sunset no planes were allowed to land, so the runway was ours.

Test 2: the first kilometres on hydrogen

The second test was maybe one of the best track tests ever, and it came right after one of the worst nights. We were in the workshop fixing problems until 2:30 AM. At 4 AM everyone was back, and we drove three hours from Delft to Circuit Zolder in Belgium, which opened at 7 AM. That day, the car drove its first kilometres on hydrogen on a real race track.

Overheating

Zolder also showed us what still needed work, most of all the cooling. After a few laps our cooling systems overheated, and that even led to errors from the IMD.

What is an IMD? An insulation monitoring device measures the resistance between the chassis and the + and − poles of the high-voltage system. If that resistance drops too low, there is a risk of electrocution, so the IMD shuts the system down. Our coolant conducts electricity slightly, and the hotter it gets, the better it conducts. So when the cooling overheated, the coolant itself started setting off the IMD.

After fixing the cooling, we did three more night tests at Breda Airport. Each time we increased the power output and solved the next set of errors.

Three tests in 36 hours

One week before the race, we still had not reached our two targets: a top speed of over 170 km/h, and 10 minutes of continuous driving without errors. So we planned three track tests in 36 hours. One crew would test at Breda Airport from 9 PM. A second crew would then drive all the equipment to Zolder and start testing there at 7 AM. Then the first crew would test at Breda Airport again from 9 PM.

The first of those tests went really well. The car went faster and faster, and it even drove for more than 10 minutes. Then, as the car drove past, all four tyres locked up. That did not look good. Over the radio I heard: "Gearbox is broken." We safely shut the car down and tried to push it by hand, but it would not move. When we opened the gearbox filters, they were full of steel and aluminium shavings. The gearbox was broken, and our preparation for the race was over.

From a race to a demo lap

But we did not give up. Racing was no longer possible, but we could still drive demo laps at TT Circuit Assen, an hour before the actual race. That gave us two days before the car had to be on display in Assen.

The car has a separate gearbox for each driven wheel, and only one of them had failed. During the day we took it apart, removed all the broken parts and searched for the cause: a loose lock ring. During the night and the early morning we put the car back together with the gearbox that still worked, and made sure its lock ring could not come loose. One last test, on an outdoor cycling track in Rotterdam that was just wide enough for our car, proved that the car could drive with only one wheel powered. Then we packed everything, drove to Assen, and prepared the car on Friday evening and night.

Race day in Assen

Then came the day we had worked towards all year: the day we could show our car. We fixed one last coolant leak on the drivetrain, lined up the car, and it drove its demo laps. It was an amazing experience, especially seeing our car cross the finish line with the previous generation driving right next to it.

My part:

  • Set up the test campaign, so that every function of the car could be validated step by step without risking major damage
  • Planned the test days towards the race, including the schedule of three track tests in 36 hours
  • Led the gearbox repair: finding the cause of the failure and getting the car ready for the demo laps in two days

What I learned

You might think that a year as Chief Engineer of a hydrogen race car mostly teaches you technical things: integration and testing, fuel cell systems, vehicle dynamics, drivetrains, suspension models or embedded systems. I learned a lot about all of those.

But what I learned most about was team dynamics. You can have the best plan ever, but in the end it is all about the people. They are the ones who execute the plan, so they have to be on board, understand it and contribute to it. Only then can you push a group of students to their absolute limits, and achieve the goal of racing the car.

The best plan is worth nothing without the people who carry it out. People are everything.
